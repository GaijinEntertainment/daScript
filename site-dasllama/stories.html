2026-09-03engine

The model that guesses its own next word.

Two models ship with a built-in guesser. We taught dasLLAMA to use it on a Mac, then spent two days finding out what the guess is worth. Less than you would hope, and about as much as llama.cpp gets.

We turned the guesser on, and Anton's Mac mini got slower. That was the morning of the 2nd of September, and it is the reason this story has a second day.

First, the trick. A language model writes one word at a time, and for a big model on a laptop every word costs the same: one pass over all of its weights, the billions of numbers that are the model. Speculative decoding is the old idea of letting something small guess a few words ahead and having the big model check the guesses in a single pass. If the guesses are right you get two or three words for the price of one pass and a bit. If they are wrong you wasted the bit. Either way the answer is the same: the big model only keeps a guessed word it would have written itself.

Two current models ship with their own guesser. Qwen 3.8 carries an extra layer, called a NextN head, that predicts the word after the next one. Gemma 4 ships a small four-layer assistant model next to the big one. Both now work in dasLLAMA on Metal, Apple's way of driving the GPU, on any Apple machine the engine runs on. In practice the guesser is a second file you load next to the model, and you get a word and a bit per pass instead of a word. We measured it on Boris's new M5 Max laptop and on Anton's M4 Pro Mac mini, which Anton lent us for the week; it lives on the other side of the globe and answers over ssh.

What we tried

How many words to guess ahead, first of all. One guess means the big model checks two words per pass. Two guesses means three, and the second guess only pays if it is right often enough to cover the third word's cost. We tried one against two on each machine and let the tuner decide - the tuner is the part of dasLLAMA that tries the alternatives on your machine and keeps what is faster - and every machine we own picked one. Three and four were so far behind on the development build that they never made it to the proper measurement.

We also looked at guessing wide instead of deep: checking the two or three most likely words at one position rather than a chain. We dropped it on paper. With only one word ahead there is no room for a runner-up, and together with depth it multiplies the work the check has to pay for.

We dropped the automatic controller too, the one that would raise the depth while the guesses keep landing, in favour of the per-machine setting. It may come back one day as a per-request setting; the math prompt below explains why.

The Gemma assistant reads what the big model was thinking as its input, and there are two places to read that from. The one at the very end of the big model's work guessed better, so that one ships. We also checked whether squeezing the big model harder hurts the guesser: the 8-bit Gemma accepted 76.5% of the guesses, the 4-bit one 74.6%. It does not, much.

And then the kernels - the small programs the GPU actually runs - which turned out to be the real story and get their own section below.

How we measured

The board on daslang.io uses llama.cpp's own benchmark tool, and that tool cannot measure speculative decoding. So we built a small ruler for ourselves. It takes four chat prompts, renders each one through the model's own chat template with the "thinking" mode off, and then runs two engines on the identical rendered text. Both engines always take their most likely word, so there is no randomness; exactly 128 new words each, three repetitions, one dated and stamped record per machine and model.

Ours runs as the release executable, the same program the board uses, with the machine's tuning picks measured fresh that same day. On Anton's M4 the ruler started it like this, for one guess ahead:

DAS_TUNE_MANIFEST= DASLLAMA_MTP_HEAD="/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" "modules/dasLLAMA/performance/_rig/dasllama-bench.app/Contents/MacOS/dasllama-bench" -- -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --mtp-ab --mtp-depth 1 -n 128 -r 3 --ngl 99 --prompts "modules/dasLLAMA/benchmarks/data/specbench4_prompts.txt" --chat-prompts

llama.cpp runs as its own llama-server, built straight from upstream at commit 6fdd0ac89, exactly as they ship it. We hand it the same rendered text over its network interface and ask it not to stop early, so it also writes exactly 128 words. The ruler started it in the background, for the same arm on the same machine, as:

"/Users/dasbox/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server" --port 18790 --host 127.0.0.1 -ngl 99 -fa on -c 2048 -t 10 -np 1 --no-warmup -fit off -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --spec-type draft-mtp --spec-draft-n-max 1 -md "/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" -ngld 99

and read the server's own timings back.

The four prompts are the first turns of four tasks from Spec-Bench (hemingkx/Spec-Bench, Apache-2.0): a travel blog post to write, a news article to summarize, a grade-school arithmetic problem (from GSM8K), and a trivia question. Both engines read the same guesser file, and the records note its fingerprint, so there is no doubt about that.

Two of the ruler's rules exist because each one cost us an afternoon. The first: same thinking mode on both sides. Our very first llama.cpp run had thinking on while ours had it off, and their guesser suddenly landed 91% of the time. A thinking-on answer is an outline, not a blog post. It is a different text with a different hit rate, so the ruler now renders one prompt and hands the same bytes to both engines. The second: let the machine cool down. A Mac that has been working hard for ten minutes writes words 13 to 18% slower than a cool one, so every arm now waits three minutes on the big 27B Qwen before it measures. And the release executable runs before anything else. When the measuring program had already run the engine itself, everything it measured afterwards came out 20% low, for reasons we still find funny.

The whole thing, on Anton's M4, is one line, and everything below reproduces from it on the dates and builds shown:

bin/daslang -jit modules/dasLLAMA/harness/mtp_ruler.das -- -m $HOME/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf --server $HOME/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server --depths 1,2 -t 10 --settle 180 -o modules/dasLLAMA/performance/records/mtp_m4_Qwen3.8-27B.json

The arithmetic of a round

Here is the part we most wanted to write down. One round with one guess produces, on average, 1 + a words, where a is how often the guess lands. So its speedup over plain decoding is (1 + a) / c, with c the cost of the round measured in plain passes. Turn it around and the tables tell you what a round costs: c = (1 + a) / speedup.

model, machine our round, in passes llama.cpp's round, in passes
gemma, M5 Max 1.75 / 1.19 = 1.47 1.79 / 1.37 = 1.31
gemma, M4 Pro 1.75 / 1.14 = 1.53 1.83 / 1.27 = 1.44
Qwen 3.8, M5 Max 1.77 / 1.20 = 1.48 1.83 / 1.35 = 1.35
Qwen 3.8, M4 Pro 1.77 / 1.10 = 1.61 1.82 / 1.20 = 1.52

Checking two words at once costs both engines about one and a half plain passes. That, not the guesser, is what caps the gain at 1.1 to 1.2x on a big dense model: the extra word is the same weights read for two inputs instead of one, plus the guesser, plus bookkeeping. On Anton's M4 the round's own clocks, with a plain pass at 78.7 ms, read: guessing 7.4 ms, checking 119.0 ms, deciding what to keep 1.0 ms, replaying the state 0.7 ms. Ninety-three percent of the round is the check.

It is also why two guesses fall behind on the M4 and only break even on the M5. A third word costs another half to a whole pass, and the second guess lands 51 to 66% of the time, which does not cover it. The math prompt is the exception. There the second guess lands 86 to 88%, and two guesses come out ahead on every machine: 187.9 against 160.1 for Gemma on the M5. How well the guesser does is a property of the text you give it, which is why the per-request setting may come back one day.

Where the two days went

Back to that morning. Anton's machine read 12.7 words per second plain and 11.0 with the guesser. llama.cpp on the same machine read 11.5 and 13.6. Without that second pair, a check that cost two full passes for two words would have looked like the price of a smaller GPU. With it, it looked like what it was. As Boris put it, it is a lot easier when you have an oracle; otherwise how do you know you are slow?

The kernel lab said the same thing in numbers. The kernel that checks two words in one pass over the weights cost 1.36 to 1.49 times two single passes on the M4 Pro, and 0.52 to 0.89 on the M5 Max. Same code, two verdicts, because the two chips have very different amounts of arithmetic per byte they can read, and this kernel is limited by arithmetic. A kernel written in the shape llama.cpp uses sat at 0.9 to 1.05 on the M4. So we wrote a new form for the M4-class chips, where each of the GPU's many small workers holds two rows of weights close at hand and feeds both from one read of the input. The tuner tries both forms on a machine and keeps the faster. The M4 took the new one, 0.48 ms against the old form's 0.84 on the test shape; the M5 kept the old one, 0.49 against 0.53. The M4 row went to 13.9.

Then Boris asked why the two-guess row was dropping like crazy: 7.9 words per second, 0.62 of plain. It was the same story one row up. Checking three words used a four-input kernel nobody had ever tried on this chip, and on the M4 it costs 1.3 to 1.5 times four single passes. We already had all the pieces - "and 1 + 2 is better than 3", as Boris said - so three words now run as the new two-row form plus one single pass, wherever the tuner picked that form. The two-guess row went from 7.9 to 12.4 and the one-guess row to 14.0 by the evening of the 3rd. About a hundred lines and a test. Finding it took a yardstick.

The yardstick

Glad we have llama.cpp next to every number here. It is the gold standard of running language models on your own machine, and it is the only way we know whether we are fast and the only way we know when to stop; the second day ended where it did because the two columns had met. Both projects are open source. Anything in this engine is theirs for the taking, with the license's attribution, and the same goes the other way. On these prompts, on these two machines, on these two days: plain decoding is 10 to 26% faster in dasLLAMA, the speculative rounds land within a few percent of each other in both directions, and llama.cpp's two-guess round is cheaper than ours. We know that one is ours to fix, and it is what winter evenings are for.

2026-08-30hardware

Boris got a new laptop.

An M5 Max, an engine that had never seen one, and sixty-two kernels that had to earn their place on it again.

Boris got a new laptop. An M5 Max, at the end of August, and it became the main machine the same afternoon. dasLLAMA - our inference engine, written top to bottom in daslang, because the language is the thing we are actually building here - had never run on an M5. Anton lent us his Mac mini, an M4 Pro of the previous generation that sits on the other side of the globe and answers over ssh, so we could watch the two chips side by side. And we got to work.

What a new chip is, to this engine

dasLLAMA tunes itself to whatever chip it lands on. For every heavy operation - a matrix multiply, an attention step, a normalization - it ships several small GPU programs, kernels, that compute the same thing in different ways. The first time it runs on a machine it tries them all, keeps the fastest of each, and writes the picks into a small file called a sidecar. Sixty-two such choices on the M5 Max. So supporting the M5 meant letting the tuner run, and then looking hard at every place where the pick had changed.

Three places did.

The M5's GPU has tensor units: dedicated matrix hardware, reached through Apple's Metal 4 tensor API. For each matrix multiply in the engine we wrote a tensor-unit version next to the plain one and let the machine choose. On the M5 Max the tensor version came out ahead in 23 of them. That covers every multiply over the compressed 4-, 5-, 6- and 8-bit weights and the 16-bit ones, the expert layers of the mixture-of-experts models, and the attention scores. The choice lives in the sidecar. A machine that never tried keeps the plain version, and one that tried and found it slower keeps it too.

On the CPU side, the M5 Max has no efficiency cores at all. It has 6 cores Apple calls Super and 12 it calls Performance, and all 18 are the real thing. On Anton's M4 we had taught the CPU lane to leave the efficiency cores out of the worker pool, because they only slowed everyone down. On the M5 that habit would have quietly thrown away a third of the chip. Now all 18 work, and token generation parks the six slower ones. We could not believe our eyes, so we checked it twice, on both machines.

The parts that look at pictures and the parts that listen run on the GPU on the M5 the way the text path does. Nothing new in the design, the same GPU plumbing, but each one had to be tried again and its picks re-made.

Tokens per second, reading a 512-token prompt and then writing 128 tokens, on Metal, measured on 2026-08-30; the full M5 Max board is on the board.

Before and after, one chip generation apart, on the same 27B model: Anton's M4 Pro reads 126.4 / 12.9, the new laptop 913.9 / 27.8. Reading the prompt got seven times faster. Writing the answer got a bit more than twice as fast - that one is limited by how quickly memory can feed the chip, and there is only so much a new chip can do about memory. llama.cpp shows the same picture, 126.3 / 11.5 on the M4 and 752.1 / 25.4 on the M5.

The parts that look at pictures ride the same machine, the same day. gemma-4-12B's image encoder, the piece that turns a picture into tokens the language model can read, takes 7.1 ms where llama.cpp's multimodal path takes 95 ms. The part of Qwen2.5-Omni that looks at images, its window vision transformer, takes 106 ms against 118 ms.

The parts that listen, too. whisper large-v3-turbo transcribes the short JFK clip that whisper.cpp ships as its sample in 140 ms, against whisper-cli's 153 ms on the same GPU. Canary-Qwen, NVIDIA's speech-to-text model, in its new 8-bit form does the same clip in 307.6 ms on the M5 and 512.9 ms on the M4, where NVIDIA's own NeMo on Torch MPS takes 704.7 and 1076.5. On a much longer clip we read 6484 ms and NeMo 4383 ms, which gives us something to do during the long winter evenings.

Glad we have llama.cpp, whisper.cpp and NeMo numbers next to ours. Those are the industry standard for this kind of thing, and they are the only way we know whether we are fast, and the only way we know when to stop. Without a yardstick a new chip is just a machine that runs, and every one of these numbers would be a little worse.

Sixty-two kernels walked into a new chip.

Most walked out the same. Twenty-three came out wearing tensor units. A few found out, in the first afternoon, that the form that had carried them for two chip generations was now the slow one, and they did not get a vote: they were rewritten before the tuner had anything worth picking. A spec sheet shows none of this. The tuner's log shows all of it, and the log is where the week went - happily, which probably says something about us.
