We turned speculative decoding on, and Anton's Mac mini got slower. That was the morning of
the 2nd of September, and it is why this story has a second day.

The idea first. A model generates one token at a time, and for a big model every token
costs a full pass over the weights. Speculative decoding lets a small drafter propose a few
tokens ahead and has the big model verify them all in one pass. Right guesses give you two
or three tokens for the price of one pass and a bit; wrong guesses cost you the bit. The
output does not change either way: the big model only keeps a drafted token it would have
produced itself.

Two current models ship with their own drafter. Qwen 3.8 carries a NextN head, one extra
layer that predicts the token after the next one. Gemma 4 ships a four-layer assistant
model next to the 26B main model. Both now run in dasLLAMA on Metal, on any Apple GPU the
engine runs on; the drafter is a second file you load next to the model. We measured on
Boris's new M5 Max laptop and on Anton's M4 Pro Mac mini, which he lent us for the week and
which answers over ssh from the other side of the globe.

## What we tried

How deep to draft, first of all. One draft token means the big model verifies two tokens per
pass; two means three, and the second draft only pays if it is accepted often enough to
cover a third row. We tried one against two on each machine and let the tuner decide, the
way it decides everything else on a box, and every machine we own settled on one. Three and
four were so far behind on the development build that they never reached the ruler.

We also looked at drafting wide instead of deep: verifying the two or three most likely
tokens at one position rather than a chain. We dropped it on paper. With one token ahead
there is no room for a runner-up, and together with depth it multiplies the rows the
verify has to pay for.

We dropped the automatic depth controller too, the one that would raise the depth while the
drafts keep landing, in favour of the per-box setting. It may come back one day as a
per-request setting; the math prompt below explains why.

The Gemma assistant reads the main model's hidden state as its input, and there are two
places to read it from, before or after the final norm. After won on acceptance, so after
ships. We also checked whether quantizing the main model harder hurts the drafter: the Q8_0
Gemma accepted 76.5% of the drafts, the Q4_K_M one 74.6%. Not much, then.

And then the kernels, which turned out to be the real story and get their own section.

## How we measured

The board on daslang.io uses llama-bench, and llama-bench cannot measure speculative
decoding. So we built a small ruler for ourselves. It renders four chat prompts through the
model's own chat template with thinking off, then runs two engines on the identical rendered
text: greedy, exactly 128 new tokens each, three repetitions, one dated and stamped record
per machine and model.

Ours runs as the release executable, the same program the board uses, on a sidecar minted
the same day - the tuner's picks for that machine, fresh. On Anton's M4 the ruler started it
like this, for depth 1:

```
DAS_TUNE_MANIFEST= DASLLAMA_MTP_HEAD="/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" "modules/dasLLAMA/performance/_rig/dasllama-bench.app/Contents/MacOS/dasllama-bench" -- -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --mtp-ab --mtp-depth 1 -n 128 -r 3 --ngl 99 --prompts "modules/dasLLAMA/benchmarks/data/specbench4_prompts.txt" --chat-prompts
```

llama.cpp runs as its own `llama-server`, built straight from upstream at commit
`6fdd0ac89`, exactly as they ship it. The ruler posts the same rendered prompt to its
`/completion` endpoint with `ignore_eos` set, so it also produces exactly 128 tokens, and
reads the server's own timings back. For the same arm on the same machine it started the
server as:

```
"/Users/dasbox/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server" --port 18790 --host 127.0.0.1 -ngl 99 -fa on -c 2048 -t 10 -np 1 --no-warmup -fit off -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --spec-type draft-mtp --spec-draft-n-max 1 -md "/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" -ngld 99
```

The four prompts are the first turns of four tasks from Spec-Bench
([hemingkx/Spec-Bench](https://github.com/hemingkx/Spec-Bench), Apache-2.0): a travel blog
post to write, a news article to summarize, a GSM8K arithmetic problem, and a trivia
question. Both engines read the same drafter file, and the records carry its checksum.

Two of the ruler's rules exist because each one cost us an afternoon. Same thinking mode on
both sides: our very first llama.cpp run had thinking on while ours had it off, and its
acceptance read 91%. A thinking-on answer is an outline, not a blog post, a different text
with a different acceptance rate; the ruler now renders one prompt and hands the same bytes
to both engines. And let the box cool down: a Mac that has been working hard for ten minutes
decodes 13 to 18% slower than a cool one, so every arm now waits three minutes on the 27B
before it measures. The release executable also runs before anything else. When the
measuring program had already run the engine in-process, everything it measured afterwards
came out 20% low, for reasons we still find funny.

The whole thing, on Anton's M4, is one line, and everything below reproduces from it on the
dates and builds shown:

```
bin/daslang -jit modules/dasLLAMA/harness/mtp_ruler.das -- -m $HOME/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf --server $HOME/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server --depths 1,2 -t 10 --settle 180 -o modules/dasLLAMA/performance/records/mtp_m4_Qwen3.8-27B.json
```

## The arithmetic of a round

Here is the part we most wanted to write down. A round with one draft token produces, on
average, `1 + a` tokens, where `a` is the acceptance rate. So its speedup over plain decoding
is `(1 + a) / c`, with `c` the cost of the round in plain passes. Turn it around and the
tables tell you what a round costs: `c = (1 + a) / speedup`.

| model, machine | our round, in passes | llama.cpp's round, in passes |
|---|---|---|
| gemma, M5 Max | 1.75 / 1.19 = 1.47 | 1.79 / 1.37 = 1.31 |
| gemma, M4 Pro | 1.75 / 1.14 = 1.53 | 1.83 / 1.27 = 1.44 |
| Qwen 3.8, M5 Max | 1.77 / 1.20 = 1.48 | 1.83 / 1.35 = 1.35 |
| Qwen 3.8, M4 Pro | 1.77 / 1.10 = 1.61 | 1.82 / 1.20 = 1.52 |

Verifying two tokens at once costs both engines about one and a half plain passes. That, not
the drafter, caps the gain at 1.1 to 1.2x on a dense 27B: the second row is the same weights
read for two inputs instead of one, plus the draft, plus bookkeeping. On Anton's M4 the
round's own clocks, against a 78.7 ms plain pass, read draft 7.4 ms, verify 119.0 ms, accept
and commit 1.0 ms, replay 0.7 ms. Ninety-three percent of the round is the verify.

It is also why depth 2 falls behind on the M4 and only breaks even on the M5. A third row
costs another half to a whole pass, and the second draft is accepted 51 to 66% of the time,
which does not cover it. The math prompt is the exception: there the second draft lands 86
to 88%, and depth 2 comes out ahead on every machine, 187.9 against 160.1 for Gemma on the
M5. Acceptance is a property of the text, not the box, which is why the per-request setting
may come back one day.

## Where the two days went

Back to that morning. Anton's machine read 12.7 tokens per second plain and 11.0 with the
drafter on. llama.cpp on the same machine read 11.5 and 13.6. Without that second pair, a
verify that cost two full passes for two rows would have looked like the price of a smaller
GPU. With it, it looked like what it was. As Boris put it, it is a lot easier when you have
an oracle; otherwise how do you know you are slow?

The kernel lab said the same in numbers. Our two-row verify kernel - one pass over the
weights serving two input rows - cost 1.36 to 1.49 times two single-row passes on the M4
Pro, and 0.52 to 0.89 on the M5 Max. Same code, two verdicts: the two chips have very
different amounts of arithmetic per byte of memory bandwidth, and this kernel is bound by
arithmetic. A kernel written in the shape llama.cpp uses sat at 0.9 to 1.05 on the M4. So
we wrote a new form for M4-class chips, a register tile where each GPU thread holds two
weight rows and feeds both from one read of the input. The tuner tries both forms on a box
and keeps the faster. The M4 took the new one, 0.48 ms against the old form's 0.84 on the
test shape; the M5 kept the old one, 0.49 against 0.53. The M4 row went to 13.9.

Then Boris asked why the depth-2 row was dropping like crazy: 7.9 tokens per second, 0.62 of
plain. Same story, one row up. Three rows took a four-column kernel nobody had ever tried on
this chip, and on the M4 it costs 1.3 to 1.5 times four single passes. We already had all
the pieces - "and 1 + 2 is better than 3", as Boris said - so three rows now run as the new
two-row form plus one single pass, wherever the tuner picked that form. Depth 2 went from 7.9
to 12.4 and depth 1 to 14.0 by the evening of the 3rd. About a hundred lines and a test.
Finding it took a yardstick.

## The yardstick

Glad we have llama.cpp next to every number here. It is the gold standard of running
language models on your own machine, the only way we know whether we are fast, and the only
way we know when to stop: the second day ended where it did because the two columns had met.
Both projects are open source. Anything in this engine is theirs for the taking, with the
license's attribution, and the same goes the other way. On these prompts, on these two
machines, on these two days: plain decoding is 10 to 26% faster in dasLLAMA, the speculative
rounds land within a few percent of each other in both directions, and llama.cpp's depth-2
round is cheaper than ours. That one is ours to fix, and it is what winter evenings are for.
