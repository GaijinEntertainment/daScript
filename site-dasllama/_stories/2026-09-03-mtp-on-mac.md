Speculative decoding is the trick where a small drafter guesses the next few tokens and the
big model checks all of them in one pass. If the guesses are good, you get several tokens
for the price of a little more than one step. Two current model families ship their own
drafter: Qwen 3.8 carries a NextN head - one extra transformer layer that predicts the
token after next - and Gemma 4 ships a four-layer assistant model beside the 26B trunk.
Both are now supported on Metal, on any Apple GPU dasLLAMA runs on. This is the story of
getting there and of measuring what it is worth.

## What we tried

**Depth.** A round drafts `k` tokens and verifies `k + 1` rows. Depth 1 is the safe bet;
depth 2 pays off only when the second guess is right often enough to cover a third verify
row. We raced depth 1 against depth 2 on each box and made the answer a box knob the tuner
mints (`mtp_depth_nextn`, `mtp_depth_assistant`). Every box we own minted 1. Depth 3 and 4
lost outright on the development rail and never reached the ruler.

**Width.** Verifying the top-2 or top-3 candidates at one position instead of a chain. We
dropped it on paper: with one token ahead there is no room for a second candidate to win,
and combined with depth it multiplies the verify rows the round cannot afford.

**A depth controller.** An online controller that raises depth while acceptance stays high.
Dropped in favour of the box knob; it may return as per-request depth, because acceptance
is a property of the prompt, not the box - the math prompt below runs 94% at position 1.

**The drafter's input.** The Gemma drafter takes the trunk's hidden state as input. Pre-norm
lost to post-norm on acceptance; post-norm ships. The target's quantization is not what caps
acceptance: the Q8_0 Gemma target accepted 76.5% against the Q4_K_M target's 74.6% on the
same corpus.

**The kernels.** Verify rows on K-quant weights ride a small-batch GEMV that streams each
weight row once for all rows. That kernel is ALU-bound, and Apple GPUs differ in how much
ALU they have per byte of bandwidth. The M5 Max likes one form; the M4 Pro needed a
different one - a register tile where each thread owns two weight rows and both activation
columns - and, above two rows, a walk of that tile over column pairs instead of the
four-column kernel. Which form a box gets is decided by a race at tune time and stored in
its sidecar, like every other kernel choice in the engine.

## How we measured

The board on daslang.io runs the `llama-bench` protocol, and `llama-bench` cannot measure
speculative decoding. So we built a ruler for ourselves: `modules/dasLLAMA/harness/mtp_ruler.das`.
It renders four chat prompts through the model's own template with thinking off, then runs
two engines on the identical rendered text, greedy, exactly 128 tokens each, three
repetitions, and writes one dated, sha-stamped JSON per box and model.

Ours runs as the release executable - the same artifact the board uses - on a sidecar
minted the same day. The ruler starts it with the box's own settings:

```
DAS_TUNE_MANIFEST= DASLLAMA_MTP_HEAD="/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" "modules/dasLLAMA/performance/_rig/dasllama-bench.app/Contents/MacOS/dasllama-bench" -- -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --mtp-ab --mtp-depth 1 -n 128 -r 3 --ngl 99 --prompts "modules/dasLLAMA/benchmarks/data/specbench4_prompts.txt" --chat-prompts
```

llama.cpp runs as a stock `llama-server` built from upstream at commit `6fdd0ac89` - no
fork, no patch - fed the same rendered prompt raw through `/completion` with `ignore_eos`,
so it generates exactly 128 tokens too. The ruler starts it, per depth `K`, as
`llama-server --port P --host 127.0.0.1 -ngl 99 -fa on -c 2048 -t T -np 1 --no-warmup -fit off -m MODEL --spec-type draft-mtp --spec-draft-n-max K -md HEAD -ngld 99`
(the function is `run_lcpp_arm`; `T` is the box's thread count, 10 on the M4 Pro) and reads
the server's own timings back.

The prompts are the first turns of four Spec-Bench tasks
([hemingkx/Spec-Bench](https://github.com/hemingkx/Spec-Bench), Apache-2.0): writing (qid 81),
summarization (qid 241), GSM8K math (qid 401) and qa (qid 321), in
`modules/dasLLAMA/benchmarks/data/specbench4_prompts.txt`. Both engines read the same
drafter file: the Qwen NextN head as `mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf`, the Gemma assistant as
`mtp-gemma-4-26B-A4B-it-Q8_0.gguf`; the JSON records carry their sha256.

Two lessons the ruler now enforces, because each one cost us an afternoon:

- **Same thinking mode on both sides.** Our first llama.cpp pass ran with thinking on and
  ours with thinking off, and their acceptance read 91%. A thinking-on continuation is an
  outline, not a blog post; it is a different text and a different acceptance rate. The
  ruler renders one prompt and hands it to both.
- **Settle before every arm.** A heat-soaked Mac under-reads a memory-bound decode by 13 to
  18%. Every arm waits 180 seconds on the dense 27B before it measures, the way the board
  driver does. And the release executable runs first: a parent process that had already run
  the engine in-process made the child's speculative arm read 20% low.

The invocation on the M4 Pro, verbatim:

```
bin/daslang -jit modules/dasLLAMA/harness/mtp_ruler.das -- -m $HOME/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf --server $HOME/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server --depths 1,2 -t 10 --settle 180 -o modules/dasLLAMA/performance/records/mtp_m4_Qwen3.8-27B.json
```

## The arithmetic of a round

Here is the honest core. A depth-1 round produces on average `1 + a` tokens, where `a` is
the acceptance rate, so its speedup over plain decode is `(1 + a) / c`, with `c` the round's
cost in plain steps. Read `c` back from the tables: `c = (1 + a) / speedup`.

| model, box | das round, in steps | llama.cpp round, in steps |
|---|---|---|
| gemma, M5 Max | 1.75 / 1.19 = 1.47 | 1.79 / 1.37 = 1.31 |
| gemma, M4 Pro | 1.75 / 1.14 = 1.53 | 1.83 / 1.27 = 1.44 |
| Qwen 3.8, M5 Max | 1.77 / 1.20 = 1.48 | 1.83 / 1.35 = 1.35 |
| Qwen 3.8, M4 Pro | 1.77 / 1.10 = 1.61 | 1.82 / 1.20 = 1.52 |

A round that verifies two rows costs both engines about one and a half plain steps, and
that - not the drafter - is what caps the gain at 1.1 to 1.2x on a 27B dense model. The two
extra rows' worth of work is the same weight pass read for two activation columns instead of
one, plus the drafter, plus the bookkeeping. On the M4 Pro the round's own clocks on
2026-09-03 (build 24d13f1d2, plain step 78.7 ms) read: draft 7.4 ms, verify 119.0 ms,
walk and commit 1.0 ms, replay 0.7 ms. Ninety-three percent of the round is the verify, and
the verify is 1.51 plain steps for two rows.

That is also why depth 2 loses on the M4 Pro and only breaks even on the M5: a third row
costs another 0.5 to 1.0 steps, and the second draft position is accepted 51 to 66% of the
time, which does not cover it. Except on the math prompt, where position 2 runs 86 to 88%
and depth 2 wins on every box (187.9 against 160.1 for gemma on the M5). Acceptance is the
prompt's property. The box knob is the right default; the prompt-driven knob is the next
idea.

## Where the day went

The M4 Pro Qwen row is the one that moved. On the morning of 2026-09-02 it read 12.7 off and
11.0 at depth 1 - speculation was a loss on that box - while llama.cpp's read 11.5 and 13.6.
The ruler said we were slow, and the ruler was the only reason we knew: on the M4's own
terms, a 2.06-step round for two rows looked like the price of a small GPU.

The lab said otherwise. Our two-row kernel cost 1.36 to 1.49x of two single-row passes on
the M4 Pro, and 0.52 to 0.89x on the M5 Max - the same kernel, two verdicts. A kernel
shaped like llama.cpp's own sat at 0.9 to 1.05x on the M4. So we wrote the two-row
register tile, raced it, crowned it on the M4 (0.48 ms against the old form's 0.84 on the
race shape) and not on the M5 (0.53 against 0.49), and the row went to 13.9. Then the
depth-2 row - 7.9 tok/s, 0.62x of plain - turned out to be the same story one row up: three
rows took the four-column kernel, which no race had ever looked at. Walking the crowned
tile over column pairs took the depth-2 row to 12.4 and the depth-1 row to 14.0 by the
evening of 09-03. The whole fix is about a hundred lines and a test; finding it took a
yardstick.

## The yardstick

llama.cpp is the gold standard of local inference and it is the yardstick in every table
here, not the opponent. Both projects are open source; they are welcome to anything in this
engine with the license's attribution, and we take anything legal from anywhere. On this
corpus, on these two boxes, on these two days: plain decode is ours by 10 to 26%, the
speculative rounds land within a few percent of each other in both directions, and their
depth-2 round is cheaper than ours. The ledger item for that last one is open.
