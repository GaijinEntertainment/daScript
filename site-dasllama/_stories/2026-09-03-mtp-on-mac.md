We turned the guesser on, and Anton's Mac mini got slower. That was the morning of the 2nd
of September, and it is the reason this story has a second day.

First, the trick. A language model writes one word at a time, and for a big model on a
laptop every word costs the same: one pass over all of its weights, the billions of numbers
that are the model. Speculative decoding is the old idea of letting something small guess a
few words ahead and having the big model check the guesses in a single pass. If the guesses
are right you get two or three words for the price of one pass and a bit. If they are wrong
you wasted the bit. Either way the answer is the same: the big model only keeps a guessed
word it would have written itself.

Two current models ship with their own guesser. Qwen 3.8 carries an extra layer, called a
NextN head, that predicts the word after the next one. Gemma 4 ships a small four-layer
assistant model next to the big one. Both now work in dasLLAMA on Metal, Apple's way of
driving the GPU, on any Apple machine the engine runs on. In practice the guesser is a
second file you load next to the model, and you get a word and a bit per pass instead of a
word. We measured it on Boris's new M5 Max laptop and on Anton's M4 Pro Mac mini, which
Anton lent us for the week; it lives on the other side of the globe and answers over ssh.

## What we tried

How many words to guess ahead, first of all. One guess means the big model checks two words
per pass. Two guesses means three, and the second guess only pays if it is right often
enough to cover the third word's cost. We tried one against two on each machine and let the
tuner decide - the tuner is the part of dasLLAMA that tries the alternatives on your machine
and keeps what is faster - and every machine we own picked one. Three and four were so far
behind on the development build that they never made it to the proper measurement.

We also looked at guessing wide instead of deep: checking the two or three most likely
words at one position rather than a chain. We dropped it on paper. With only one word ahead
there is no room for a runner-up, and together with depth it multiplies the work the check
has to pay for.

We dropped the automatic controller too, the one that would raise the depth while the
guesses keep landing, in favour of the per-machine setting. It may come back one day as a
per-request setting; the math prompt below explains why.

The Gemma assistant reads what the big model was thinking as its input, and there are two
places to read that from. The one at the very end of the big model's work guessed better, so
that one ships. We also checked whether squeezing the big model harder hurts the guesser:
the 8-bit Gemma accepted 76.5% of the guesses, the 4-bit one 74.6%. It does not, much.

And then the kernels - the small programs the GPU actually runs - which turned out to be
the real story and get their own section below.

## How we measured

The board on daslang.io uses llama.cpp's own benchmark tool, and that tool cannot measure
speculative decoding. So we built a small ruler for ourselves. It takes four chat prompts,
renders each one through the model's own chat template with the "thinking" mode off, and
then runs two engines on the identical rendered text. Both engines always take their most
likely word, so there is no randomness; exactly 128 new words each, three repetitions, one
dated and stamped record per machine and model.

Ours runs as the release executable, the same program the board uses, with the machine's
tuning picks measured fresh that same day. On Anton's M4 the ruler started it like this, for
one guess ahead:

```
DAS_TUNE_MANIFEST= DASLLAMA_MTP_HEAD="/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" "modules/dasLLAMA/performance/_rig/dasllama-bench.app/Contents/MacOS/dasllama-bench" -- -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --mtp-ab --mtp-depth 1 -n 128 -r 3 --ngl 99 --prompts "modules/dasLLAMA/benchmarks/data/specbench4_prompts.txt" --chat-prompts
```

llama.cpp runs as its own `llama-server`, built straight from upstream at commit
`6fdd0ac89`, exactly as they ship it. We hand it the same rendered text over its network
interface and ask it not to stop early, so it also writes exactly 128 words. The ruler
started it in the background, for the same arm on the same machine, as:

```
"/Users/dasbox/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server" --port 18790 --host 127.0.0.1 -ngl 99 -fa on -c 2048 -t 10 -np 1 --no-warmup -fit off -m "/Users/dasbox/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf" --spec-type draft-mtp --spec-draft-n-max 1 -md "/Users/dasbox/models/mtp-Qwen3.8-27B-Q8_0.gguf" -ngld 99
```

and read the server's own timings back.

The four prompts are the first turns of four tasks from Spec-Bench
([hemingkx/Spec-Bench](https://github.com/hemingkx/Spec-Bench), Apache-2.0): a travel blog
post to write, a news article to summarize, a grade-school arithmetic problem (from GSM8K),
and a trivia question. Both engines read the same guesser file, and the records note its
fingerprint, so there is no doubt about that.

Two of the ruler's rules exist because each one cost us an afternoon. The first: same
thinking mode on both sides. Our very first llama.cpp run had thinking on while ours had it
off, and their guesser suddenly landed 91% of the time. A thinking-on answer is an outline,
not a blog post. It is a different text with a different hit rate, so the ruler now renders
one prompt and hands the same bytes to both engines. The second: let the machine cool down.
A Mac that has been working hard for ten minutes writes words 13 to 18% slower than a cool
one, so every arm now waits three minutes on the big 27B Qwen before it measures. And the
release executable runs before anything else. When the measuring program had already run the
engine itself, everything it measured afterwards came out 20% low, for reasons we still find
funny.

The whole thing, on Anton's M4, is one line, and everything below reproduces from it on the
dates and builds shown:

```
bin/daslang -jit modules/dasLLAMA/harness/mtp_ruler.das -- -m $HOME/models/Qwen3.8-27B-Q4_K_M.gguf --server $HOME/llama.cpp-ref-6fdd0ac/build-stock/bin/llama-server --depths 1,2 -t 10 --settle 180 -o modules/dasLLAMA/performance/records/mtp_m4_Qwen3.8-27B.json
```

## The arithmetic of a round

Here is the part we most wanted to write down. One round with one guess produces, on
average, `1 + a` words, where `a` is how often the guess lands. So its speedup over plain
decoding is `(1 + a) / c`, with `c` the cost of the round measured in plain passes. Turn it
around and the tables tell you what a round costs: `c = (1 + a) / speedup`.

| model, machine | our round, in passes | llama.cpp's round, in passes |
|---|---|---|
| gemma, M5 Max | 1.75 / 1.19 = 1.47 | 1.79 / 1.37 = 1.31 |
| gemma, M4 Pro | 1.75 / 1.14 = 1.53 | 1.83 / 1.27 = 1.44 |
| Qwen 3.8, M5 Max | 1.77 / 1.20 = 1.48 | 1.83 / 1.35 = 1.35 |
| Qwen 3.8, M4 Pro | 1.77 / 1.10 = 1.61 | 1.82 / 1.20 = 1.52 |

Checking two words at once costs both engines about one and a half plain passes. That, not
the guesser, is what caps the gain at 1.1 to 1.2x on a big dense model: the extra word is the
same weights read for two inputs instead of one, plus the guesser, plus bookkeeping. On
Anton's M4 the round's own clocks, with a plain pass at 78.7 ms, read: guessing 7.4 ms,
checking 119.0 ms, deciding what to keep 1.0 ms, replaying the state 0.7 ms. Ninety-three
percent of the round is the check.

It is also why two guesses fall behind on the M4 and only break even on the M5. A third word
costs another half to a whole pass, and the second guess lands 51 to 66% of the time, which
does not cover it. The math prompt is the exception. There the second guess lands 86 to 88%,
and two guesses come out ahead on every machine: 187.9 against 160.1 for Gemma on the M5.
How well the guesser does is a property of the text you give it, which is why the
per-request setting may come back one day.

## Where the two days went

Back to that morning. Anton's machine read 12.7 words per second plain and 11.0 with the
guesser. llama.cpp on the same machine read 11.5 and 13.6. Without that second pair, a check
that cost two full passes for two words would have looked like the price of a smaller GPU.
With it, it looked like what it was. As Boris put it, it is a lot easier when you have an
oracle; otherwise how do you know you are slow?

The kernel lab said the same thing in numbers. The kernel that checks two words in one pass
over the weights cost 1.36 to 1.49 times two single passes on the M4 Pro, and 0.52 to 0.89
on the M5 Max. Same code, two verdicts, because the two chips have very different amounts
of arithmetic per byte they can read, and this kernel is limited by arithmetic. A kernel
written in the shape llama.cpp uses sat at 0.9 to 1.05 on the M4. So we wrote a new form
for the M4-class chips, where each of the GPU's many small workers holds two rows of weights
close at hand and feeds both from one read of the input. The tuner tries both forms on a
machine and keeps the faster. The M4 took the new one, 0.48 ms against the old form's 0.84
on the test shape; the M5 kept the old one, 0.49 against 0.53. The M4 row went to 13.9.

Then Boris asked why the two-guess row was dropping like crazy: 7.9 words per second, 0.62
of plain. It was the same story one row up. Checking three words used a four-input kernel
nobody had ever tried on this chip, and on the M4 it costs 1.3 to 1.5 times four single
passes. We already had all the pieces - "and 1 + 2 is better than 3", as Boris said - so
three words now run as the new two-row form plus one single pass, wherever the tuner picked
that form. The two-guess row went from 7.9 to 12.4 and the one-guess row to 14.0 by the
evening of the 3rd. About a hundred lines and a test. Finding it took a yardstick.

## The yardstick

Glad we have llama.cpp next to every number here. It is the gold standard of running
language models on your own machine, and it is the only way we know whether we are fast and
the only way we know when to stop; the second day ended where it did because the two columns
had met. Both projects are open source. Anything in this engine is theirs for the taking,
with the license's attribution, and the same goes the other way. On these prompts, on these
two machines, on these two days: plain decoding is 10 to 26% faster in dasLLAMA, the
speculative rounds land within a few percent of each other in both directions, and
llama.cpp's two-guess round is cheaper than ours. We know that one is ours to fix, and it is
what winter evenings are for.
